What fair playing time means for a volunteer coach

Most recreational leagues ask for something like equal playing time, and every parent has a different idea of what that means. Some count innings on the field, some count at-bats, some count how often their child gets a turn at the interesting positions. The part a coach controls most directly is the bench: who sits, and when. If the bench is even, everything else gets easier to defend.

This generator makes the bench the first decision of every game. For each inning it works out how many players must sit (the number of available players minus the number of positions), then chooses them from the players who have sat the least so far. A player who sat last inning is only picked again if there is no alternative. The positions are filled afterwards.

The two bench rules

  • Bench fairness. Nobody sits a second time before everyone available has sat once. Among players present for the same innings, bench totals differ by at most one.
  • No sitting two innings in a row. A child who sits innings 3 and 4 watches a third of a six-inning game from the dugout. Spreading the bench stops that, as long as the roster allows it.

When the numbers make it impossible, for example a player locked to pitcher for four innings who therefore cannot sit early, the chart still appears. Under it, the rule check says which rule was broken and for whom, in plain words, so you can decide whether to change a lock or accept it.

Use the fairness summary

Under the chart, the fairness summary lists every player with innings played, bench innings, infield innings and the positions they played. It updates as soon as you swap two cells by hand. A small warning mark appears beside any player whose row breaks a rule you have switched on, and the message above names the rule. If you save the game, the season view keeps adding these numbers, which is useful when a parent asks whether their child has played their share this year.

Be honest about the limits

Every rule in LineupFair is a coach setting, not league policy. Leagues differ on minimum playing time, batting orders, pitching and substitutions, so check your league's rules before you rely on a chart. Equal bench innings are not the same as equal quality of playing time, and the tool cannot judge it for you. It also does not track at-bats or pitch counts. Use it to make a fair starting chart quickly, then adjust it with what you know about your team.

Frequently asked questions

What does equal playing time mean in this tool?

It means bench innings are even. Among players who are there for the same innings, the number of innings on the bench differs by at most one, and nobody sits a second time before everyone available has sat once. It does not measure skill, and it does not count at-bats.

Why can bench innings differ by one?

Because the slots rarely divide evenly. With ten players and six innings of Standard 9 there are six bench slots for ten players, so six players sit once and four do not sit at all. A difference of one is the smallest possible.

Does a late arrival count as sitting?

No. A player who arrives in inning 3 is not on the bench in innings 1 and 2; they are simply not there yet. Their bench innings are compared only with players who were present for the same innings.

Can I make the bench time exactly equal by turning off other rules?

Bench fairness is already the first thing the tool settles. Turning off the other rules gives it more freedom for positions, but it cannot create bench slots that do not divide evenly.

Will parents see a record of playing time?

You can print the dugout card or save games to a season tally that adds up bench innings, infield innings and positions per player. Share the tally only with people you choose; nothing is posted online.
